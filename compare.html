Compare the first three years

How did the economy and public finances fare in the first three years of each of the last three presidents? Each president's party or coalition won a parliamentary majority in his first year, so each had a similar chance to carry out his plans.

This page does not declare a winner. The result depends on what you think matters most and on how much allowance you give for crises. Change the settings below and see how the comparison moves. Every figure comes from official statistics, and every calculation is explained at the bottom of the page.

How this comparison works

Periods. All three presidents took office in mid-November, so we compare their first full calendar years. These are 2014 to 2016 for Abdulla Yameen, 2019 to 2021 for Ibrahim Mohamed Solih, and 2024 to 2026 for Mohamed Muizzu. The year before each term (2013, 2018 and 2023) is the starting point for changes.

Data. All figures come from the MMA Statistics Database. Government finances use the MMA's annual government finance statistics for every year, so all three presidents are measured the same way. Inflation uses the national consumer price index, tourism uses monthly tourist arrivals, reserves use official reserve assets, and external debt uses central government external debt in US dollars, converted at the MMA rate and divided by GDP. For 2026, revenue, spending and GDP are official projections, and tourism, prices and reserves cover January to the latest month, compared with the same months of 2025.

Scores. Each indicator is scored from 0 to 100 among the three presidents. The overall score is the weighted average of the indicator scores. Because only three presidents are compared, small differences can look large under "By size of gap", which is why "By position only" and the robustness check are offered.

The tourism adjustment

Tourism drives the Maldivian economy, and tourist numbers swing for reasons no government controls, such as pandemics and global recessions. We measured how closely growth follows tourism using 21 years of data (2005 to 2025), by fitting a straight line through real GDP growth against growth in tourist arrivals (ordinary least squares).

We only adjust where the data show a strong link, which we set at an R² of 0.5 or more. Growth passes this test easily. The budget balance and the current account do not, because governments respond to tourism slumps in very different ways, so adjusting them would rest on guesswork. For those, the fairer allowance is to leave crisis years out, which the switches above let you do.

We use log changes, the standard way to measure growth when changes are large. In log terms a 67% crash followed by a full recovery adds up to zero, which ordinary percentages do not. The "check only" row shows the link is almost as strong without the pandemic years, so it is not a COVID effect alone. With the adjustment on, each year's growth becomes

ln(1 + adjusted growth) = ln(1 + actual growth) − 0.37 × (ln(1 + tourism growth) − typical tourism growth)

The typical tourism growth is the median year, 8.9%. The median is used because a few extreme years would distort an average.

Tourist arrivals growth itself is then left out of the score, since it is the very thing being adjusted for.

The oil price adjustment

The Maldives imports all its fuel, so world oil prices move the current account. With the adjustment on, each year's fuel bill is recalculated as if oil had been at its long-run average, and the difference is taken out of the current account.

adjusted current account = current account + fuel imports × (1 − average oil price ÷ that year's oil price) ÷ GDP

This takes away Yameen's advantage from cheap oil in 2015 and 2016, and the costs Muizzu faced from expensive oil in 2026, using the same rule for both.

The indicators in detail

    Limits

    • Three years is a short window, and some results reflect decisions made by earlier governments, such as interest on debt they inherited.
    • The adjustments cover the largest outside forces (tourism and oil) but not everything, such as world interest rates, which rose sharply before Muizzu took office.
    • Leaving out crisis years means judging a president on fewer years.
    • Borrowing decisions can show up years later, as repayments and interest paid by a later government. A three-year window can't capture that fully.
    • Muizzu's 2026 figures are projections and will be replaced with actual results as they are published.
